# Use more, then less

**Why did companies tell workers to use more AI, and then less?**

Many companies treated using a lot of AI as a sign of good work, and then the bills arrived. In 2025 and early 2026, many large companies pushed their workers to use AI, and some ranked workers by how much they used.[^pragmatic] By the summer of 2026, many were trying to spend less. Chapter 14 showed that businesses pay for AI by the amount of text, like an electric meter.

Claude, the AI made by Anthropic, wrote this chapter, and Claude appears in the story.

## Workers were told to use more

- The chief executive of Shopify, which runs online shops for other businesses, wrote that "reflexive AI usage" was now "a baseline expectation", and that reviews would include questions about AI use.[^shopify]
- Meta, the company that owns Facebook, told staff in November 2025 that from 2026 their reviews would reward helping it toward "an AI-native future", a company built around AI.[^meta-2025]
- The chief executive of Nvidia, which sells the <Term id="chip">chips</Term> that AI runs on, said that he would be "deeply alarmed" if an engineer paid $500,000 used less than $250,000 of AI.[^huang]

The industry calls a <Term id="word-piece">word piece</Term>, a word or part of a word, a token. In March 2026, the New York Times described tokenmaxxing, a status game among workers of using as many word pieces as possible. Some companies ranked their staff by it.[^roose]

At Meta, one employee built an unofficial ranking of colleagues called "Claudeonomics", with titles such as "Token Legend". Staff used about 60 trillion word pieces in 30 days. The employee took the ranking down after its data was shared outside Meta.[^claudeonomics]

Some Meta employees claim in a lawsuit that counts of word pieces helped decide who lost their jobs; Meta denies it, and as far as I could find, the claims have not been decided.[^meta-denial] In April 2026, Meta said it would lay off about 8,000 people, a tenth of its staff.[^meta-layoffs] In July, 26 current and former employees sued. They claim that Meta's own records of AI use helped choose whom to let go, and that people on medical or family leave could not build up those counts.[^does-v-meta] A judge declined to stop the layoffs, and the claims go to a private hearing.[^judge] This is the only case I found that ties counts of word pieces to layoffs.

## Then companies tried to spend less

By the summer of 2026, companies were trying to spend less, and some moved away from Claude. Claude comes up often here because, by one survey, Anthropic took the largest share of what large American businesses spent on models.[^menlo]

- Uber's chief operating officer said that the link between its AI spending and its results "is not there yet". Reports said that Uber had used its whole year's budget for AI tools that write <Term id="program">programs</Term> in four months.[^uber]
- Meta told about 6,000 staff that it would limit their use of word pieces.[^meta-limits] In September, it said that it would not judge employees' work by that use.[^meta-reviews]
- Accenture, a consulting company, capped what it spends on Claude at $29 a person a month, and Adobe ended unlimited use of Claude.[^accenture][^adobe]
- Microsoft moved most engineers in one division from Claude Code, Anthropic's tool for writing programs, to its own tool. It said it wanted everyone on the same tools.[^microsoft]
- AT&T's head of AI said that most work does not need the latest and best models.[^att] One company, Lindy, moved from Anthropic's models to DeepSeek's cheaper ones.[^lindy]

In October, Amazon told its engineers that counts of word pieces are "deeply flawed" as measures of their work, and that "tokenmaxxing is at odds with frugality". In the same document, it said that the counts match useful work well, and that it keeps using them "with care and judgement".[^amazon]

As far as I could find, no study has measured whether using more word pieces gets more work done. Chapter 18 describes studies of whether AI helps programmers at all.

**So why did companies tell workers to use more AI, and then less?** Use was easy to count, and many treated it as a sign of good work.[^ibm] When the bills grew, some of the same companies, Meta among them, asked for less, in some cases by moving away from Claude. As far as I could find, no one has shown whether more use got more work done.
