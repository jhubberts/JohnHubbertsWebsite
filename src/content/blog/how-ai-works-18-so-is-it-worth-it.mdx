# So is it worth it?

**So is it worth it?**

My mom asked me five questions, and the answers came with costs and cautions. I meant every one of them. I also use AI systems all day, by choice. In this chapter, I say why. What follows is mine: I wrote it in my own words to Claude, the AI system that wrote this series, and Claude made it plainer.

## One day, in the background

This whole series was made in one day, while I did my regular job. Now and then I looked over, read what Claude had done, and typed a line or two.

## The cost of trying

I could have built every piece of this by hand. I know how to make a website. I know how to copy a <Term id="model">model</Term> onto my computer and <Term id="run">run</Term> it. I know how to build something like the drawing box in chapter 4.

It would have taken many hours of tedious work. For a present to one reader, I would never have started.

Trying an idea used to cost me so much time that most ideas were not worth it. Now it costs me almost no time, so I try them. It still has the costs that chapter 13 counted.

My work has changed the same way. My company has fewer than fifty people. I believe we now do what would have taken a few hundred people a few years ago. That is my own judgment; I have not measured it. Chapter 13 has the other side of it: the people who are not hired.

## It is not only me

People who write <Term id="program">programs</Term> for a living report the same change, and some warnings.

- In mid-2026, 90 in 100 professional programmers used AI <Term id="agent">agents</Term> at work at least once a week. 68 in 100 used them every day.[^jetbrains-agents]
- In 2025, a trial with 16 programmers found that experienced programmers took 19% longer with AI, while believing it had made them 20% faster.[^metr-2025] The researchers tried again in early 2026 and could not get a reliable answer, though they now think programmers are faster.[^metr-2026]
- In a 2025 survey, more programmers distrusted the accuracy of AI's work (46 in 100) than trusted it (33 in 100).[^stack-overflow-trust]
- In the United States, job postings for programmers are about a fifth lower than in early 2020.[^fred-postings] They rose from May 2025 to May 2026, and most of that rise was for experienced people.[^indeed-senior]

That trial is a warning about claims like mine. Those programmers felt faster and were slower, and I have not measured myself.

## What I give up

I spend much of my day talking to AI systems, and less of it in a room with the people I work with. I miss that.

<JohnsThoughts>
  This incentivized isolation and de-humanization is the risk of AI that I feel most acutely day to
  day. I think as a society we'll converge on a healthy balance, but it will take time and it will
  be a massive pain in the ass. Our humanity is what makes us special and nothing can or will ever
  take that from us.
</JohnsThoughts>

As I said in chapter 17, I give these systems more trust than I can fully defend.

I also cannot tell you what this one day used in electricity. As chapter 13 said, I could not find a published figure from the company that makes Claude.

## Who else can do this

Someone who never learned to write code, the instructions a computer follows, could get most of the way to a series like this by asking in plain sentences, as I did. Learning it took me years of school, and it was hard. They would get stuck where I knew what to look for: the page that slid under my finger, and the news story the AI had missed, both in chapter 17.

You could be that someone. A plain <Term id="chat-system">chat</Term> is the place to start. As chapter 12 said, it can only write text to you.

<JohnsThoughts>
With that said, the barrier of entry to do something amazing with computers has fallen so it's accessible to nearly anyone. As long as you're not writing bank software or building medical devices, making something that's a little bit wrong is totally fine.

The other side is truly amazing though. We live in a world dominated by technology. For better or for worse (mostly worse), nearly everyone on earth is connected to the internet every day. The internet is this massive open equalizing space, but even 10 years ago you needed a lot of education to take a creative idea and turn it into something real. Maybe you're an amazing artist or writer or musician...if you wanted to make a website or use technology to help with your ideas you needed to hire someone to help or learn a ton. Maybe you're someone with a math or logic learning disability who doesn't do well with programming, but you know exactly what you'd want in a website if you can make one.

All of those people have the power to create. I have the power to create on a whim, and because I know how computers work, I can describe some profoundly crazy things that I want to create and not get mired down in the mechanical details.

This is the true power of the age we're in. We have opened a pandora's box with many nasty side effects, but it affords everyone the ability to invent on a whim.

When I was really young I said when I grew up I wanted to become an inventor. I grinded STEM for my whole life, went to engineering school, cut my teeth for a decade working at Amazon building and sometimes inventing...but holy smokes am I inventing today, and at a pace I've never experienced in my life.

</JohnsThoughts>
