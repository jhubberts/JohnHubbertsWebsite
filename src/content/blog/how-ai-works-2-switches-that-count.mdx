# Switches that count

**How does a machine hold a number?**

As a row of switches. Each switch is either off or on, and a row of them can stand for any whole number. Every computer since the 1940s holds its numbers, its letters and its <Term id="program">programs</Term> this way.

<EightSwitches />

Off is written 0 and on is written 1. A single switch is called a bit.[^openlearn] Counting with only 0 and 1 is called binary. Each switch to the left is worth twice the one to its right, so eight switches cover every number from 0 to 255. A computer stores a letter as one of those numbers.

## Turing's machine on paper

In 1936, a 23-year-old English mathematician, Alan Turing, described a machine that existed only on paper. It read and wrote symbols on a long paper tape, one square at a time, and decided what to do next from a table of <Term id="rule">rules</Term>.[^turing-1936]

Turing also described a single machine that could carry out any other machine's table of rules, if that table was written on its tape.[^turing-1936] One machine, given different instructions, could do any job that instructions can describe. A computer today is that kind of machine: one machine that <Term id="run">runs</Term> many different programs. Charles Babbage had planned this for his Analytical Engine, the machine in chapter 1.

During the Second World War, Turing worked at Bletchley Park, the British codebreaking center, and, with Gordon Welchman, designed the Bombe, a machine that sped up breaking the German Enigma code.[^gchq] In 1950 he asked "Can machines think?" and proposed a test, now called the Turing test, of whether a person chatting in writing can tell a machine from a human.[^turing-1950]

## The first electrical switches: relays and tubes

A year after Turing's paper, Claude Shannon, a student in the United States, showed that circuits of electrical switches could do logic, meaning decisions such as "if this and that, then do this", and arithmetic.[^shannon] The first switches were relays, small electromagnets that clicked a contact open or shut. Then came vacuum tubes, glass bulbs that switch with no moving part but get hot and burn out.

ENIAC, a computer built in the United States, was finished in 1945 and shown to the public in 1946. It used close to 18,000 vacuum tubes, filled a room 30 by 50 feet, weighed 30 tons, and could do one addition in 1/5,000 of a second.[^eniac] In 1955 it ran for 5.6 hours, on average, before something failed.[^eniac-1961]

Babbage's store from chapter 1, the part that holds numbers, is now made of switches like these. So is his mill, the part that does the arithmetic.

## The transistor and the chip

In December 1947, two physicists, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain, built the first <Term id="transistor">transistor</Term>. They worked in a group led by William Shockley at Bell Labs, the research laboratory of the Bell telephone company in New Jersey. A transistor is a switch controlled by electricity: a small current at one contact turns a larger current on or off.[^transistor] It is smaller than a tube, uses less power, and lasts far longer. The three men shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics.[^nobel-1956]

In 1958 Jack Kilby, an engineer at the American electronics company Texas Instruments, made several parts of a circuit on one piece of material. In 1959 Robert Noyce, another American engineer, found a practical way to make such <Term id="chip">chips</Term>. Both men are counted as inventors of the integrated circuit, which is now called a chip.[^chm-ic]

In 1965, Gordon Moore, a chip engineer, noticed that the number of transistors and other parts on a chip was doubling every year. In 1975 he changed that to every two years.[^moore][^chm-moore] The doubling went on for decades, as these three chips show. Each is named for the company that made it: Intel, Apple and NVIDIA.

| Chip                                                   | Year | Transistors |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ---- | ----------- |
| Intel 4004, an early chip for calculators[^intel-4004] | 1971 | 2,300       |
| Apple M4, in an iPad[^apple-m4]                        | 2024 | 28 billion  |
| NVIDIA Blackwell, built to run AI[^blackwell]          | 2024 | 208 billion |

<JohnsThoughts>
  Around the time I started my adventure into learning about computing, Moore's law began to fail
  due to thermodynamic limitations of transistors. Today transistors get smaller every year, but the
  exponential growth of yore is long dead.
</JohnsThoughts>

A chip like these works through billions of steps a second.[^intel-clock] In each of those steps, the chip does something as small as adding two numbers or comparing them. Everything a computer does, including the AI in the rest of this series, is a long program of such small steps.

**So, how does a machine hold a number?** As a row of switches, each on or off. A chip holds billions of them, switching billions of times a second. Chapter 3 turns to AI, starting with jobs where nobody can write the program's rules.
