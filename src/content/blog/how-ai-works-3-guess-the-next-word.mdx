# Guess the next word

**How does it write?**

One word at a time. The part of an AI system that works with words is called a language model. Like the network in chapter 2, it is a large set of numbers found by practice, and it does a single thing. Given some text, it rates every word that could come next.

You already own a small one. When you type a message on your phone, it offers you the next word above the keyboard. Try the same game against a real model.

<YouBeTheAutocomplete />

## What you just saw

Each time, the model gave a number to every word that could come next. You picked one, and it started again from the longer text.

When you have played a round, look at the last row, "Every other word put together". It is often a large share. The model is rarely sure.

That one step is all the writing is. A page of text from a chat system, such as ChatGPT or Claude, is this step, repeated hundreds of times.

## Who picks the word?

The model only gives the ratings. A separate, simple step then picks one word, like rolling dice that are weighted by the ratings. That step is ordinary computer code, and one setting controls how far it may stray from the top word. If the top word is always taken, the writing goes flat and repeats itself. If less likely words are taken sometimes, it gets livelier. If they are taken too often, it turns to nonsense.

<TheDice />

The word is chosen fresh each time. That is why the same question can get two different answers.

## Words as numbers

A model cannot work with letters. Inside it, every word is a list of numbers. The model on this page uses 768 numbers for each word. No single number has a meaning that anyone gave it. What matters is which words have similar lists.

Nobody chose those numbers. They were found the way the numbers in chapters 1 and 2 were found: guess, check, adjust. Here the guess was the next word, and the examples were eight million web pages.

Words that are used in similar ways ended up with similar numbers. Psychologists know something like this from word-association tests, where "dog" brings "cat" to mind. Opposites often land close together too, because they are used in the same kinds of sentence. Try "sad".

<WordMap />

The model on this page is called GPT-2. OpenAI released it in 2019. It is small and old, and its writing wanders. The model that followed it a year later, GPT-3, had about 1,400 times as many numbers in all.

**So how does it write?** It rates every word that could come next. One word is chosen, and the model starts again. Everything a chat system writes is made this way.
